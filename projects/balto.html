Building a Qualitative Data Pipeline for Transit Research

At BALTO Community Data Labs, I built and maintained the pipeline that turned interviews, workshops, and stakeholder conversations about Baltimore's public transit system into a structured, traceable research dataset.

TL;DR
  • Problem: Transit data can tell you a bus was late, but not what that means for the person who had to build an extra hour into their day around it.
  • What I did: Built and maintained the qualitative coding pipeline behind BALTO's Baltimore transit-equity research: 50+ interviews, 100+ documents, and a coding framework I built and refined.
  • Key finding: The most useful patterns weren't complaints, they were specific, actionable mechanisms, like residents repeatedly asking "what happens after we speak?"
  • Impact: Turned hundreds of unstructured conversations into a structured, traceable dataset that could actually inform community advocacy and policy recommendations.

The problem

Transit data can tell you when a bus arrived. It can't tell you whether a rider had to build an extra hour into their day because it's always late, or whether someone felt respected while using the system.

BALTO's Community Data Labs exist to capture exactly that gap, and the richest information came from unstructured sources: interviews, workshop sessions, transit-agency debriefs, surveys, audio recordings, and hundreds of individual discussion prompts. My corpus mapping alone covered 221 prompts across community sessions, advocate interviews, and MTA/BCDOT conversations. The real challenge was turning that volume of human, messy material into a consistent dataset without stripping out the context that gave it meaning.

What I did

I worked across the full pipeline (collection, organization, coding, validation, and synthesis) on two guiding research questions: what makes a community data lab actually center community concerns and advocacy (RQ1), and how useful is BALTO's toolkit for addressing real transit problems (RQ2).

  • Reviewed and cleaned 50+ interviews and workshop transcripts, and 20+ hours of recorded material
  • Cross-checked flagged quotes against original transcripts to verify speaker, timestamp, and wording
  • Built and refined the qualitative coding framework: codes, subcodes, definitions, and examples
  • Wrote research memos connecting evidence to emerging themes and research questions
  • Mapped hundreds of prompts back to the project's research questions so findings stayed traceable to their source

Where the analysis got interesting

Similar isn't the same

"Community values" and "rider dignity and rights" can look interchangeable in a single conversation, and if the boundary between codes stays vague, two researchers will code the same quote differently. I reviewed quotes in context, compared them against existing definitions, and added subcodes where a broad category was hiding a real distinction.

New codes emerged from what residents were actually asking

A recurring pattern wasn't just "this route is unreliable"; it was residents asking "what happens after we speak?" That became its own code, Trust & Accountability Loop. Others followed the same logic: Engagement Infrastructure (the mechanisms that keep residents connected: QR codes, contact channels, recruitment), Operational Trade-Offs (the tension between adding a stop and slowing down a route), and Embedded Observation (the idea that officials should experience a rider's actual journey, not just hear about it).

The new codes pointed toward mechanisms, not just problems

"Transit unreliability" tells you what's wrong. Trust & accountability, engagement infrastructure, and operational trade-offs start to explain what it would actually take to move from a community complaint to an institutional response. That shift, moving from cataloging problems to identifying mechanisms for change, was the most useful thing the coding work produced.

A dashboard can represent community experience accurately and still fail the community if the people it's meant for can't access or use it. Building the tool isn't the finish line; getting the insight into the hands that need it is.

Themes that came out of the coding

Reliability

More than "was the bus late": it's the planning burden of building extra time into every trip, and how that ripples into access to work and healthcare.

Accessibility

Physical (stop location, transfers), informational (can you understand the schedule), and digital (can you actually use the app): three different barriers hiding under one word.

Rider dignity

Equity isn't only "can you get there"; it's also "how were you treated while getting there." One memo specifically called for clearer definitions of "dignity" and "respect" so advocates wouldn't translate those ideas too loosely.

Trust & accountability

Participation doesn't automatically build trust. Residents can answer the same survey repeatedly and still have no idea whether their answers changed anything.

A note on confidentiality

BALTO's research agreement keeps the underlying data (transcripts, questionnaires, raw analyses) under the faculty supervisors' control. This case study doesn't include any raw quotes or participant data; everything above is described at the process and methodology level, which is also just good research practice.

What I learned

Qualitative data can be every bit as structured as a spreadsheet if the coding system is disciplined, though the codebook still shapes what you're able to see. If categories stay too broad, genuinely different experiences collapse into the same label, and you lose the distinction that actually matters. Traceability isn't optional either: every quote used in a finding needs to trace back to its speaker, timestamp, and source, or the finding isn't defensible.

What I'd do differently

  • Define code ownership, definitions, and overlap rules before large-scale coding starts, not after inconsistencies show up
  • Add formal inter-coder reliability checks (like Cohen's kappa) alongside team discussion, to quantify consistency rather than just talk about it
  • Keep a versioned codebook (version, definition, date, reason for change) so the evolution of the framework is auditable
  • Pair qualitative themes like "perceived unreliability" against operational metrics like on-time performance, to see where the two align or diverge
