About Me

Hi, I'm Brenda.

PU

I'm interested in people: how we make decisions, what we need from the systems around us, and how technology can make those systems better rather than simply more advanced.

That curiosity has taken me in a lot of directions. I've worked with AI and machine learning, studied how people interact with technology, analyzed investment and community data, and built tools meant to turn complicated information into something people can actually use. On paper, those experiences look pretty different. To me, they're connected by the same question: how do we build things that work better for the people who depend on them?

I grew up in Rwanda, and some of my earliest ideas about opportunity came from seeing how much a person's circumstances shape what feels possible to them. That's part of why I studied Information Science at Cornell for both my undergraduate and master's degrees. It gave me room to write code and build models while still asking questions about design, ethics, and how power and access actually work. I've never cared about technology for its own sake. I care about what happens when it leaves the screen and becomes part of someone's everyday life.

That shows up in how I approach my work. I like data, but I'm interested in the person behind the data point. I like AI, but I care just as much about whether someone can understand it, trust it, or know when not to rely on it.

Outside of work, I'm usually trying to get better at something I'm bad at, lately that's boxing and learning to ride a bike much later than most people do. I also spend a lot of time keeping up with AI policy and the questions emerging alongside the technology: how governments are responding to increasingly capable systems, what responsible regulation should look like, who should be accountable when these systems fail, and how we protect people without standing in the way of useful innovation.

I'm especially interested in the gap between what AI can do and what we ultimately decide it should do. I don't think those decisions belong to technology alone, which is why I care about staying close to the people, policy questions, and real-world consequences that first made me curious about this work.

If you're thinking about similar problems, I'd love to talk.
